📖 How to Play

Classic Snake is the timeless arcade game that ruled a generation of phones: steer a growing snake around the board, eat the red apples, and don't crash. Every apple makes you longer and adds to your score, so the better you play, the harder it gets. Your high score is saved automatically.

  1. Press Start. Tap "Start Game" and your snake begins moving across the board on its own — your job is purely steering.
  2. Steer with your favourite controls. On a keyboard, use the arrow keys. On a phone or tablet, swipe across the board in the direction you want to go, or tap the arrow pad under the board.
  3. Eat the red apples. Guide the snake's head onto an apple to eat it. Each apple scores 10 points and adds one segment to your tail.
  4. Don't crash. The game ends if your head touches a wall or any part of your own body — including the tail that keeps growing behind you.
  5. Chase the high score. Your best score is saved in your browser and shown above the board. Press Start again after a game over to try to beat it.

🏆 Pro Winning Tips

  • Hug the walls early. While your snake is short, travel around the edges of the board. The middle is where traps form; the perimeter always leaves you an escape route.
  • Plan two turns ahead. Don't just steer at the apple — look at where your head will be after eating it, and make sure that spot has an exit.
  • Never chase your own tail closely. Following directly behind your tail is safe only while it keeps moving away. Leave at least a square or two of breathing room.
  • Make wide, lazy loops. Big sweeping circuits around the board are far safer than tight zigzags, which fold your body into a knot you can't escape.
  • Slow is smooth, smooth is fast. The snake moves at a steady speed that never changes, so panic is the real enemy. Calm, early turns beat last-second swerves every time.

🧠 Strategy

The classic Snake strategy is to treat the board as a racetrack. Run a big loop around the outside, and only dive into the middle when an apple appears there — then return to your loop as soon as you have eaten it. This keeps most of your body trailing neatly behind you in a predictable line instead of scattered across the board in coils that block your path. As your snake grows past 15 or 20 segments, space becomes the whole game: before every apple run, quickly check that the route in and the route out both stay open.

Late in a game, survival beats greed. An apple in a tight pocket between your body and the wall is often a trap — eating it can seal the pocket with you inside. Let it go; another apple will spawn somewhere safer. Also learn the one move that saves runs: when you are about to hit your body, turn alongside it rather than away from it, following your own side until a gap opens. And remember, the snake cannot reverse — a 180-degree turn into your own neck is blocked by the controls, but a quick double-turn (up then left, for example) achieves the same deadly result, so give your turns room to breathe.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

How do I play Snake on my phone?

Tap Start, then either swipe on the game board in the direction you want the snake to go, or tap the arrow pad below the board. Tapping a direction on the pad also starts the game if it isn't running yet.

How does scoring work in Classic Snake?

Every apple you eat is worth 10 points and makes your snake one segment longer. There is no time bonus — your score is simply the number of apples eaten multiplied by ten.

Is my high score saved?

Yes. Your highest score is stored in your own browser's local storage and displayed above the board, ready to be beaten on your next visit from the same device and browser.

Does the snake get faster as I eat more apples?

No — the speed stays constant for the whole game. What changes is your length: a longer snake leaves less room to manoeuvre, which is where the real challenge comes from.

Why did the game end when I turned quickly?

Quick double-turns can steer your head into your own body even though a direct 180-degree reversal is blocked. Space your turns out slightly so each one registers before the next.
