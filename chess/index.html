How to Play Chess vs Computer

  1. You play White and always move first. Tap one of your pieces to select it: legal moves appear as dots on empty squares and red rings around pieces you can capture.
  2. Tap a highlighted square to complete the move. The computer then thinks briefly and replies as Black.
  3. Captured pieces are displayed in the rows beside the board, so you can always see the material balance — what you have captured and what you have lost.
  4. All special moves are supported: castle by moving your king two squares toward a rook, capture en passant when an enemy pawn passes yours, and pawns that reach the last rank promote automatically to a queen.
  5. Your king flashes red when it is in check, and you must get out of check on your move. The game ends with checkmate, stalemate or a draw.
  6. Tap New Game at any time to resign and start fresh. Your record — wins, losses and draws — saves automatically in your browser.

Pro Winning Tips

  • Fight for the center. Open by pushing a central pawn (e4 or d4) and aim your pieces at the four middle squares — central control gives your army room to move and restricts the computer.
  • Develop before you attack. Bring knights and bishops out, then castle, before launching anything ambitious. An attack with two pieces against a full army almost always fails.
  • Castle early. Castling tucks your king behind a wall of pawns and connects your rooks in one move. Delaying it is the most common beginner mistake.
  • Check before every move: "what does that square attack?" Most games at this level are decided by pieces left hanging. Before you confirm a move, make sure the landing square is not simply defended by an enemy piece.
  • Keep knights central. A knight in the middle attacks up to eight squares; a knight stranded on the rim attacks as few as two. "A knight on the rim is dim" is a cliché because it is true.
  • Trade when you are ahead, refuse when you are behind. If you are up material, exchanging pieces simplifies toward a winning endgame. If you are down, keep pieces on and complicate.

Chess vs Computer Strategy

Beating the computer consistently is less about brilliance than about not blundering. The engine plays a solid, club-style game: it develops sensibly, grabs free material instantly and punishes loose pieces, but it will not out-calculate a careful human over a long game. Use the standard material values — pawn 1, knight and bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9 — and glance at the captured rows before any exchange to be sure the trade favours you. In the opening, follow the classic recipe: one or two central pawn moves, knights before bishops, castle by move ten, and connect your rooks. In the middlegame, improve your worst-placed piece and look for simple tactics — forks (one piece attacking two), pins (a piece that cannot move without exposing something bigger) and discovered attacks decide most games. When you win a piece, switch to simplification mode: offer queen trades, exchange rooks, and march the extra material home. In the endgame, activate your king — it becomes a fighting piece once the queens are gone — and push passed pawns with king support. Played patiently, this no-nonsense approach wins far more games than flashy sacrifices that the computer will simply refute.
